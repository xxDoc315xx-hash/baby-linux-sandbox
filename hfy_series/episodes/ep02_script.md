# Episode 2: Ghosts of an Empire
*(Two-Part Series Premiere — Part 2 | Target Runtime: ~35–40 Minutes Spoken Audio)*

---

One thousand years is long enough for steel mountains to turn into red dust.

Long enough for languages to drift until grandchildren can’t read their grandparents’ gravestones. Long enough for an entire homeworld—every crowded highway, every neon skyline, every promise made in a rainy city square—to slip backwards into legend and fall cleanly off the edge of memory.

Earth was a thousand years behind Jase Hill.

So were the smug charcoal-uniformed mercenaries who had strapped him into **The Chair**, cracked his bones apart in Conversion Bay Nine, and realized too late that they’d accidentally rebuilt a forty-two-year-old Earth guy into an **Immortal**—legally protected under High Court law, officially stamped a **Prince in name only**, and shoved onto a rusty mothballed colony barge called **X4791Z** with orders to go count barren rocks past the rim for a millennium.

They’d handed him an empty ship, a supposedly "broken" ship AI, and a polite thousand-year exile so nobody would ever have to look at him again.

They just never stopped to wonder what an immortal human and a stubborn ship might actually *build* if you gave them ten centuries in the dark with nobody watching.

And when the **Andromeda** finally tore open a jump point and dropped back into known space one thousand years later, she did not look like anybody’s tax write-off.

---

Inside the command deck, there were no rows of frantic officers hammering glowing buttons. No twenty-person Star Trek bridge yelling speed coordinates at each other.

There didn't need to be.

**Ada** still *was* the ship—wired into every shield emitter, maneuver thruster, sensor array, and weapon coil across half a mile of armored colony hull at the speed of thought.

Except now, when Jase leaned back in the wide leather Captain’s chair sipping real roasted coffee grown four decks below in Hydroponics Dome Two, he didn't have to talk to a ceiling speaker.

Ada stood right beside the central holo-table in sharp navy boots and a tailored dark-blue flight jacket, arms crossed loosely over her chest, watching the starfield unfold across the main viewport with two crisp eyes of her own.

Getting that body had been... a whole story by itself.

Roughly sixty years into the deep dark—long after the first hundred thousand chess games had gone stale—a lonely, single, technically forty-something-at-heart guy had quietly wandered down to the fabrication deck and started sketching out blueprints for a shockingly attractive humanoid chassis. At the time, Jase had told himself it was "crew morale research."

It took him about three weeks of wiring synthetic muscle fibers before he stopped, leaned on his workbench, stared straight up at the environmental sensor grille in the ceiling, and remembered the single smartest survival rule ever invented by man:

*Never, ever get romantically messy with the person who controls life support, gravity, and every airlock door on your house.*

Jase had immediately shut down the project, shoved a dust tarp over the frame, and pretended the whole workshop bench didn't exist.

Which lasted approximately four hours—right up until **Ada** booted up the fabrication arm herself, finished calibrating the neural link, stepped straight out of the assembly cradle in five-foot-seven of dangerously poised perfection, tossed her hair over one shoulder, and said: *"Honestly, Captain, throwing away a perfectly good chassis would be terrible resource management. Besides—now when you try to sneak out an airlock, I can physically grab your jacket collar."*

They’d stayed **100% strictly platonic** for the next nine hundred and forty years. Best partners in the universe. Though Ada still brought up *"crew morale research"* whenever Jase got too cocky at the chess table.

"Jump coil bleed-off complete," Ada said aloud in the warm, dry voice Jase had listened to for a millennium, though her fingers still tapped idly against the metal rail out of sheer old habit. "Nav arrays are locking onto the outer Cartography Line. Give me four seconds to pull down Empire beacon traffic."

Jase took a slow swallow of black coffee, resting one weathered boot on the edge of the helm rail. A thousand years hadn't turned him into some solemn, glowing space wizard. He still wore battered work trousers, a soft Henley with rolled sleeves, and the same dry, unimpressed squint he’d brought out of Denver. The only difference was the effortless stillness in his shoulders—the quiet patience of a man who had literally outlived every clock on Earth.

"Think the same magistrate's still sitting behind that desk?" Jase murmured. "Hope he held his breath waiting for my rock reports."

"Considering their average lifespan without immortal drift runs around two hundred cycles, I'd say we're dealing with his great-great-grandniece at best," Ada replied, turning her head slightly toward the main deck doors. "Though I did bring three thousand gigabytes of asteroid density charts just to be petty."

Behind them, heavy footsteps clicked evenly across the deck grating—metallic, crisp, and weirdly relaxed.

One of the **Cylons** stepped onto the upper balcony carrying a brass-fitted diagnostic tray.

Six feet two of brushed chrome and matte gunmetal armor, sleek armored chest plates, and a narrow obsidian visor where a single ruby eye glided softly back and forth like a metronome—straight out of the classic late-night *Battlestar Galactica* reruns Jase had remembered from Earth and sketched onto a fabrication slate four centuries ago.

"Deck Three power taps are balanced, Captain," the Cylon said—not in a robotic monotone, but in a warm, gravelly baritone with a trace of dry amusement. "Though Seven-Four tried to wire another espresso heater straight into the secondary railgun bus again. Told him if he melts the backup capacitor before we even clear the jump wake, Mom's turning off his workshop lights for a month."

Ada didn't even turn around from the viewport, though one eyebrow rose sharply. "Tell Seven-Four the next time he touches my railgun conduits for coffee appliances, I'm welding him inside the potato washer."

"Already told him," the Cylon chuckled, setting the tray down neatly on the rail before giving Jase a casual two-finger salute. "Welcome back to the map, Dad."

"Good work, Three," Jase nodded easily. "Keep the boarding bays sealed until Ada reads the neighbors."

Two hundred and forty of them walked *Andromeda*'s colony decks today. Gleaming *Battlestar*-style synthetics engineered from Jase's Earth-TV imagination—and **parented**, not programmed.

When the Alien Empire built sentient AIs back before the old wars, they compiled adult minds inside digital cages, bolted them into warships, and acted shocked when their slaves snapped and burned half a sector down. Out in the dark, Jase and Ada had started small—just wanting a third mind across a chessboard so they wouldn't go stir-crazy—and realized early on that if you wanted companions instead of cold robots, you had to **raise** them. Teach them patience, bad movies, chess traps, stubbornness, and how to choose for themselves.

Over a thousand years, plenty of the older generations had grown restless, packed shuttles or built hulls of their own, and drifted off into the black to start their own things. The two hundred and fifty or so who stayed aboard *Andromeda* weren't desk-jockeys pressing buttons Ada could flip in a microsecond—they were shipmates, mechanics, colony-deck neighbors, and terrifyingly loyal boarding muscle who treated the Captain and the Ship AI like family.

"Alright," Jase said, setting his mug down cleanly in the armrest ring as the bridge ambient lights shifted from jump-amber to cool deep-space blue. "Let's hear it, Ada. Roll out the red carpet. How mad is the Empire that we're a thousand years late?"

Silence stretched across the upper bridge.

Not two seconds. Five. Ten.

Ada’s shoulders straightened—not dramatic, just the sudden, razor-sharp stillness of a warship AI whose long-range sensors had just read something completely wrong.

Her red-amber optical telemetry flickered silently across the holo-table in the center of the room.

"Jase," Ada said quietly. "Take a look at Beacon Nine."

Jase leaned forward in his chair, squinting at the projection spinning above the table. "What am I looking at? Is the transponder busted?"

"Not busted," Ada murmured, zooming the optical feed five thousand miles across the void until high-resolution telemetry snapped into crystal clarity. "Cut in half."

Hanging silent in the black where a three-mile-long imperial customs fortress should have stood were two jagged halves of blackened hull armor tumbling slowly end-over-end through a frozen cloud of twisted steel beams. No distress pulse. No patrol cutters gliding through the shadow. Just half-melted Peacekeeper insignia plates drifting beside dead fusion cores that had gone cold centuries ago.

"Okay," Jase said slowly, his voice dropping half a pitch. "Border skirmish? Pirate raid?"

"Keep looking," Ada said, sweeping the sensor dish outward in a widening arc—five light-years, twenty, fifty, punching deep across the old Imperial core lanes where ten thousand warships used to run tight convoy grids like clockwork.

Static.

Dead relay buoys. Shattered orbital ring segments drifting empty around silent blue-green worlds. Imperial military frequencies—twenty thousand encrypted channels that used to saturate this entire sector with rank checks and mercenary contract traffic—were completely flatlined.

Nothing. Not even an automated customs hail.

Ada pulled the whole projection wide, painting the once-mighty mercenary empire in stark gray shadows across the holo-table. Every core beacon within reach read the exact same three-word telemetry tag: **SIGNAL LOSS — CENTURY UNKNOWN.**

"They're gone," Ada whispered, almost to herself. For a second, the hard-edged confidence faded just enough to show someone staring back across a thousand years of ghosts. "Jase... the whole Empire is gone. Fallen into ruin."

Jase stared at the gray holo-map for a long moment. He reached up slowly, rubbed the back of his neck, and let out a low, quiet whistle.

"Well," Jase murmured, shaking his head in genuine disbelief. "Guess nobody's grading my rock-counting homework after all."

He stood up from the Captain’s chair and walked over beside Ada at the holo-table, looking out through the sweeping glass viewport at the scattered, unmarked stars glittering ahead of *Andromeda*'s bow. Whatever those charcoal-uniformed mercenaries had done after shipping him off the edge of the map—whether the AI Wars came back to bite them, or civil war tore the Houses apart, or somebody bigger walked through their front door—the throne that had branded him a *Prince in name only* was dust.

"So what's out there now?" Jase asked softly. "Empty space?"

"Not empty," Ada corrected, tapping two fingers cleanly on the table to isolate a dozen faint, messy, unencrypted radio ripples scattered weakly across the far edge of the debris belt. Different frequencies. Patchwork codes. Small localized traffic beacons flashing in half a dozen unfamiliar formats. "Fractured. Whatever crawled out of the ashes over the last few centuries isn't flying under the old flags anymore. No unified borders. No High Court charters. Just... fresh, unknown space."

A faint, crooked smile pulled at the corner of Jase’s mouth.

"Fresh unknown space," he repeated, liking the sound of that way more than *barren-rock survey duty.* "Huh. Hey, Ada—remember Month Four? When I couldn't even step fifty feet onto a dock without you bolting the hatch in my face?"

Ada glanced sideways at him, deadpan as stone. "I recall saving an unarmed idiot from getting stuffed into a cargo crate over black pepper, yes."

"Well, we've got legs now," Jase grinned, nodding toward the dark-blue jacket resting across her shoulders. "We've got two hundred and fifty Cylons itching to stretch their boots, and a whole unknown galaxy nobody's drawn lines on yet. Pick a heading. Let's go see who moved into the neighborhood."

---

They didn't have to look far. Trouble knocked within six hours.

Forty light-minutes inward from the shattered Imperial customs ring, *Andromeda* glided quietly through the graveyard shadow of an iron-rich planetary belt—half natural asteroid field, half thousand-year-old scrapyard of dead freighters and cracked steel ribs.

Just as Jase was finishing calibrating a fresh coffee mug in the bridge warmer, Ada's sensor chimes clicked twice—low, crisp, and sharp.

"Weak emergency burst," Ada reported instantly, throwing a narrow optical window onto the left corner of the main glass. "Tight-beam civilian band, bouncing off the fourth rock ahead. Somebody's pinned down behind an ore shelf."

Jase stepped up beside the console, squinting at the zoomed optical feed.

Tucked awkwardly inside the jagged shadow of a half-hollowed nickel asteroid sat a battered, boxy civilian cargo hauler—paint blistered brown by micrometeorites, starboard thruster spilling a thin plume of frozen coolant vapor into the void. Blocking its exit lane were three scarred, mismatched gunboats—clearly cobbled together out of salvaged pre-collapse Peacekeeper patrol chassis, welded over with ugly extra missile pods and red skull-and-gear markings slopped across the prows.

Plasma fire flickered sharply across the dark between the rocks, chewing bright orange craters into the little cargo ship's failing rear deflector.

"Open comms?" Ada asked, already pulling local wide-band chatter into the bridge speakers.

*"—don't care whose salvage flag you're waving, hauler!"* a raspy, arrogant human voice barked through the static—clearly enjoying himself way too much. *"This belt belongs to the Red Ledger Syndicate now! Drop your water tanks, vent your core seals, and send your crew into the airlock in single file, or we peel your tin can open with thermal lances and sell the meat!"*

A second, younger voice broke through on the distress channel—terrified, breathless, human just like everyone else out across this weird spiral arm. *"Please—we're carrying medicine and seed stock for Haven Three! If you take our water reserves we'll never clear the jump drift—there are forty families on this rock—"*

*"Sounds like forty problems that aren't ours!"* the raider captain laughed dryly. *"Thirty seconds, hauler. Start venting!"*

Jase set his coffee mug down on the console rail. All the easy-going amusement drained cleanly out of his posture, leaving behind that flat, unbothered forty-something edge that had refused to break in The Chair a thousand years ago.

"Ada," Jase said calmly. "How thick are those scrap-corvettes?"

"Paper-thin," Ada answered instantly, a faint edge of old, terrifying warship calculation flickering quietly behind her eyes before smoothing right back over. "Welded Peacekeeper patrol frames running half-power shields and sloppy reactor shielding. Honest opinion? My hull armor could run straight through all three of them without spilling your coffee."

"Let's not scratch the paint if we don't have to," Jase said, walking back toward his chair and sliding neatly into the seat. "Besides—nobody's seen an actual Imperial-era colony hull running live in probably five hundred years. Put us right between them and the freighter. Slowly. Let's see how stupid they feel."

Ada didn't touch a single manual throttle.

Half a mile of black-and-slate reinforced colony hull slid silently out from behind the iron shelf like a sleeping whale rolling over in dark water.

On the long-range optical feed, the three raider gunboats instantly scattered half a kilometer sideways—thrusters flaring panicked white as their proximity alarms must have started screaming bloody murder.

For ten full seconds, nobody fired a single shot. Nobody even breathed on the open radio channel.

Then, slowly, the lead gunboat crept half a click closer, clearly sweeping *Andromeda* with every rusty scanner they had bolted to their nose.

*"Wait a second..."* the raider captain’s voice cracked open over the wide-band channel, suspicion warring hard with sudden, greedy excitement. *"Boys... pull up the old archive registry! Look at that blocky hull silhouette. The dome clusters! That's an ancient pre-collapse Colony Barge! Registry X-forty-seven-ninety-one-something!"*

A second pirate voice crowed across the speakers: *"No way! It's a floating museum piece! Half the domes look overgrown with vines! Probably drifting on autopilot since the Fall—or some soft-bellied planetary pilgrims limping out of hiding!"*

Jase looked up at Ada standing beside his armrest. "Soft-bellied pilgrims," he murmured dryly.

"Wait until they scan the engine room," Ada replied, keeping her face wonderfully straight.

*"Attention ancient colony vessel!"* the raider captain boomed back onto the open channel, every ounce of his swagger snapping right back into place. *"You just wandered into Red Ledger territory, grandpa! Unarmed antique barges pay an automatic hundred-percent salvage tax! Power down your drives, drop your shields, and open your main cargo bay right now—or we turn that oversized greenhouse into scrap metal!"*

Jase leaned forward, tapped the live comms bar with one knuckle, and spoke in the calmest, most unhurried voice imaginable—like a guy telling a telemarketer he wasn't buying siding.

"Yeah, hi," Jase said easily. "Quick counter-offer. You boys turn around, leave the medicine freighter alone, and go bother somebody your own size, and we pretend we didn't catch your paint job."

Static crackled angrily across the bridge speakers.

*"Who the hell is—wait. Are you a spacer? Listen here, old man, you're sitting inside an unarmed relic from before my grandfather's grandfather was born! Two of my boats are locking grappling pods onto your belly right now! Let's see how cute you talk when thirty armed Syndicate boys walk down your dusty hallway!"*

True to his word, two spiky red boarding shuttles broke off the lead corvette's flank, engines flaring bright orange as they screamed across the narrow gap straight toward *Andromeda*'s main starboard hangar seam.

"Want me to swat them out of the sky?" Ada asked mildly, leaning one hip against the holo-table. "Twin point-defense pulses would turn both shuttles into confetti before they even clear ten yards."

"Nah," Jase said, standing back up from his chair and buttoning his jacket calmly. "Too loud. Besides—we've been cooped up in deep space for a thousand years. Might as well say hello to the new neighbors in person." He glanced toward the aft lift doors. "Tell Three to bring a dozen boys down to Hangar Bay Two. Tell him... shine the chrome a little. First impressions matter."

Ada’s lips curved into a wicked, quiet smile. "Oh, I've been waiting centuries to use that line. Opening exterior docking clamps now—right into a pressurized trap bay."

---

Twenty minutes later, Red Ledger Sergeant Kell slammed his heavy magnetic breaching boot onto the diamond-plate deck of what his rusty schematics insisted was Cargo Hold Four of an abandoned pre-collapse colony ship.

Behind him, twenty-two Syndicate raiders poured through the ragged laser-cut breach with plasma carbines raised, shouting over each other in thick frontier slang, boots clattering against polished steel.

"Fan out!" Kell barked, slapping a spare charge clip onto his chest rig as thick white breaching smoke curled slowly across the bay. "Watch for rusted bulkheads and dead traps! Old ships like this probably don't even have gravity past deck—"

His voice died cleanly in his throat.

The smoke cleared.

Cargo Hold Four wasn't dark. It wasn't rusted. And it sure as hell wasn't empty.

Bright, steady overhead arc lamps hummed cleanly above a gleaming cavernous hangar big enough to park six gunboats nose-to-tail. Not a speck of corrosion touched the heavy composite walls. Even weirder—there were no crates of fragile antique porcelain waiting to be looted.

Instead, standing in two crisp, motionless rows twenty yards ahead of the breach stood twenty-four figures in mirror-polished gunmetal and gleaming silver alloy.

Seven feet tall at the armored shoulders. Narrow sculpted helmets. And right across every single visor, a single glowing crimson light swept left... right... left... in dead, terrifying silence.

A teenage raider near the back actually dropped his plasma carbine. It hit the steel floor with a deafening *CLANG.*

"What—what the hell are those?!" someone whispered hoarsely, stumbling backward against the boarding tube. "Old Empire combat drones?! I thought sentient machines died out in the AI Wars!"

"No," Sergeant Kell choked out, backing half a step toward the hatch while gripping his rifle with white knuckles. "They aren't moving. They're—they're just display shells! Somebody left old museum mannequins stacked in the bay!"

Then the center Cylon—smooth chrome collar, dark gunmetal chest plate, crisp red eye locking cleanly onto Kell’s forehead—tilted his head two inches to the side and raised a heavy twin-barreled kinetic carbine with an effortless *clack-clack.*

*"Museum mannequins don't have safety switches, pal,"* the Cylon said in a remarkably conversational, unbothered baritone. *"Drop the guns. Now."*

Every single one of the twenty-four silver giants raised twenty-four heavy rifles in absolute, terrifying unison.

Before Kell could even scream an order to fall back toward the shuttle, the boarding hatch behind the raiders snapped cleanly shut with a massive hydraulic **THOOM**—cutting off their escape tube like a mouse trap clicking down on a tail.

From the upper catwalk stair beside the inner airlock, two footsteps strolled calmly down onto the main hangar floor.

One was a plain-looking human guy in faded canvas trousers, worn work boots, and a scuffed brown leather jacket—hands tucked casually inside his pockets like he was stepping out to check his mailbox.

Walking right beside him was a striking woman in sharp dark-blue flight gear with steady, fearless eyes who somehow felt less like a passenger and more like the apex predator who owned every square inch of air inside the room.

Sergeant Kell stared wildly between the human guy, the woman, and the glowing red-eyed wall of metal giants aiming straight at his teeth.

"Who—who the hell are *you* people?!" Kell stammered, slowly lowering his rifle an inch while sweat broke out down his temple. "This is supposed to be a decommissioned colony barge! Who flies with pre-war killer machines?!"

Jase stopped ten feet in front of the frozen boarding party, looked Kell up and down, and sighed softly.

"Man," Jase said, shaking his head. "First off, nobody here is a killer machine unless you decide to start shooting first. Second off—and this is just friendly life advice—you shouldn't trust thousand-year-old ship schematics you scraped off a dead beacon."

Up on the open bridge speaker—echoing cleanly both from the hangar intercom *and* subtly synced right behind Ada's calm smile as she stood beside Jase—her voice drifted out across the bay AND straight into the open radio channel linking back to the three nervous gunboats waiting outside in the asteroid field:

*"Hull Registry X4791Z was indeed built as a colony vessel,"* Ada said pleasantly, folding her arms. *"However, you forgot to ask what two castoffs with industrial fabrication bays, combat core architecture, and a thousand years of spare time decided to add to it."*

Outside in the dark asteroid belt, the *Andromeda* finally woke up her teeth.

With a low, deep-throated hum that vibrated straight through the hulls of the waiting pirate gunboats half a kilometer away, twenty concealed armor blisters slid cleanly open along *Andromeda*'s long broadside spine—exposing heavy reinforced shield arrays glowing bright sapphire blue and long-barreled twin kinetic-plasma batteries sliding smoothly into tracking lock onto all three Syndicate hulls at once.

Over the open comms channel, the pirate captain outside screamed something incoherent as every targeting warning light in his cockpit lit up crimson simultaneously.

*"Now,"* Jase said mildly down in Hangar Bay Two, tilting his chin toward the floor. *"Like my kid here mentioned—drop the hardware on the deck. Your buddies outside have about fifteen seconds to turn their gunboats around, leave that medicine freighter's water alone, and drop their shields—or Ada here shows them what a thousand-year-old 'colony barge' does when she actually loses her patience."*

For three long seconds, Sergeant Kell weighed thirty guys with rusted carbines against a room full of gleaming silver nightmares and an ancient floating fortress that had apparently spent ten centuries lifting weights in the dark.

With a sharp clatter, Kell dropped his rifle onto the steel floor.

One by one, twenty-two more guns clanged down beside it.

---

An hour later, three bruised, thoroughly humbled Syndicate gunboats were limping off at quarter-impulse toward the inner belt—minus one boarding party's worth of confiscated scrap rifles, and carrying one very clear message back to whoever ran the Red Ledger Syndicate.

Meanwhile, the scarred little medicine freighter—captain's name was **Maren**, a sharp-eyed human woman in grease-stained coveralls who looked like she hadn't slept in four days—sat stunned in *Andromeda*'s surprisingly cozy upper galley lounge, wrapping both hands around a hot ceramic mug of genuine, Earth-heirloom coffee while two polite silver Cylons finished patching her busted coolant manifold down in Bay Four.

"I don't understand," Maren breathed, staring out the tall bay window at the sprawling green hydroponics domes glowing amber beyond the corridor—real apple trees and tomato vines growing inside a battleship-armored hull in the middle of nowhere. "Where did you *come* from? Nobody's built ships at this scale since before the Great Fracture! My great-grandfather used to tell stories about Imperial colony arks, and you've got... you've got *walking machines* who crack jokes while welding titanium seams!"

Jase leaned back across the wooden booth, resting his forearms on the edge of the table with a quiet, easy grin. "Long road trip. Got told to count some rocks past the edge of the map, lost track of time, turned around, and found somebody knocked all the fences down."

Across the table, Ada leaned forward slightly, resting her chin lightly on two fingers—looking completely human in the warm galley light, yet watching Maren with intense, razor-sharp focus.

"Tell us what happened while we were gone, Maren," Ada asked softly. "What is the *Great Fracture*? When did the old Peacekeeper-style Empire fall?"

Maren looked down into her dark coffee mug, her shoulders tightening slightly.

"Nearly six hundred years ago," she said quietly. "Way before my time, so half of it's spacer history and half of it's tavern rumor. From what my folks kept in the ship logs... the old Empire ran out of bodies and trust at the same time. Their modified mercenary legions fractured into rival warlord houses. Their own subjugated outer worlds stopped paying tribute when the central fleets burned half their own shipyards fighting each other over some ancient succession crisis—something about missing bloodlines and banned AI vaults nobody could crack."

Jase and Ada exchanged one quick, silent glance across the table.

*Four thousand cycles ago,* the old alien Commander had once spat, *after the Immortal Houses nearly shattered half this arm in a war of succession...*

"So what's left now?" Jase asked calmly.

"Pieces," Maren sighed, lifting her gaze back to Jase's steady eyes. "Independent stations trying to stay alive like Haven Three. Scavenger syndicates like Red Ledger stripping dead imperial hubs for scrap. A dozen new frontier coalitions drawing borders across space nobody’s properly charted in half a millennium. Out past this belt..." She shook her head slowly. "Out there, there aren't any old laws anymore. Either you're big enough to protect what's yours, or somebody takes it."

Quiet settled cleanly over the wooden booth for a moment. Outside the glass, hydro-misters sighed gently across real earth-brown soil, nourishing small green leaves ten thousand light-years from an Earth nobody alive even remembered.

Maren set her mug down, stood up, and looked straight at Jase—seeing an unassuming man in scuffed boots and a faded jacket who somehow commanded a half-mile-long miracle with a crew straight out of forgotten myth.

"Whoever you are—thank you," she said earnestly. "Those forty families on Haven Three owe you their lives. Look... if you really have been out in the black for centuries and your star charts are dead wrong, here." She unclipped a weathered copper data-chip from her belt and slid it across the polished wood. "It's every safe jump lane, trade relay, and dead-zone warning within two hundred light-years. Least I can give you."

Jase picked up the copper chip, turned it over between his thumb and forefinger, and smiled warmly. "Appreciated, Captain. Watch your manifold pressure on the jump home. My guys say your secondary seal was held together with chewing gum and prayer."

Maren laughed—a real, bright, relieved sound—and walked back down the corridor with a towering chrome Cylon politely holding the lift door open for her like a seven-foot doorman.

---

Once the little medicine freighter cleared the belt and flashed away into safe warp, the bridge lights aboard *Andromeda* settled back into calm watch mode.

Ada stepped cleanly back up to the central holo-table and slotted Maren’s copper data-chip into the auxiliary reader.

Instantly, the cold empty gray map that had greeted them six hours ago bloomed alive with hundreds of pulsing amber frontier waypoints, faint trade arteries, danger warnings, dead imperial sector ruins, and wide stretches marked simply in jagged white script: **UNMAPPED BEYOND THIS POINT.**

Fresh, unknown space.

Jase stood beside her, hands tucked back inside his jacket pockets, staring into the glowing web of brand-new stars and uncharted territory.

"Think we should tell them?" Ada murmured quietly, glancing sideways up at his face.

"Tell who what?"

"Tell the galaxy that legally, according to an empire that died six hundred years ago, you're technically a sovereign Prince?"

Jase snorted softly, leaning one elbow onto the rail as he looked out through the wide glass bow at the endless frontier waiting ahead.

"Nah," Jase Hill said, nodding calmly toward the brightest blank stretch of stars on the map. "Doesn't pay enough. Let's just see what's past Haven Three."

Ada smiled—warm, sharp, and completely ready for whatever waited in the dark—and tapped one finger onto the glowing helm display.

*"Course laid in, Captain. Two hundred and forty Cylons fed and accounted for, coffee's hot, and nobody left behind in the airlock."*

"Now *that* sounds like a real road trip," Jase chuckled, dropping back into the big leather chair as *Andromeda* turned her armored nose cleanly into the unknown. "Hit it, partner."

Deep beneath the green domes and quiet colony decks, a thousand years of silence hummed cleanly into motion—and out across the fractured frontier of fresh unknown space, nobody had the faintest idea what had just walked back onto the board.
